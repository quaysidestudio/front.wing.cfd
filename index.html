01 · Validation

Checking the method on a known aerofoil

Before trusting any results on my own wing, I modelled the Selig S1223 high-lift aerofoil on its own (250 mm chord, 30 m/s, Re ≈ 5.1×10⁵) and compared Fluent's k-ω SST results with XFoil at three angles of attack.

Why the offset? The lift-curve slope agrees within 9%, but Cl sits 13–16% low and Cd about 30% high at every angle. XFoil predicts laminar flow over the first 33–42% of the upper surface. k-ω SST assumes the boundary layer is turbulent everywhere, which raises skin friction and thickens the boundary layer at the trailing edge. A consistent, explainable offset like this means the setup is behaving properly.
02 · Setup

My wing, domain and mesh

The wing uses the S1223 profile, inverted for downforce, as three elements: a 250 mm main plane and two flaps scaled to 60% and 40%. In 2D it sits above a moving ground in a domain 12.5 m long and 5 m tall. The 3D model is an 800 mm half-span with a 10 mm endplate, solved with a symmetry plane on the centreline.

Problems I had to solve

Solution blew up
SymptomForces grew to 10¹⁸²; continuity stuck at 10² even with first-order schemes.
FixInflation layers on the 12.5 m ground made cells with an aspect ratio of ~18,000. The ground moves with the flow, so it doesn't need a boundary layer. I removed it from inflation.
2nd order diverged
SymptomSwitching to second order produced velocities of 242,000 m/s.
FixTwo sliver cells (skewness 0.997) at a flap leading edge in the slot, where inflation layers were crushed together. Cutting the layers from 20 to 15 brought max skewness to 0.68.
Student cell limit
SymptomThe first 3D mesh had 4.2 M cells. The licence refuses anything over 1,048,576.
FixCoarser surface sizing and 5 thicker inflation layers (y⁺ ≈ 20–50, which SST handles) got it to 875k.
3D gave lift, not downforce
SymptomEvery element showed negative downforce and negative drag, at about ten times the expected size.
FixThe inlet and outlet faces were swapped, so the air ran backwards. I now check each face's centroid before naming it.
03 · Mesh independence

Is the answer the mesh or the physics?

I ran the 50 mm case on three meshes, changing only the wing surface spacing. Net downforce changed by 6.7% from coarse to medium, then only 2.2% from medium to fine. The changes are shrinking, so the solution is converging. Extrapolating suggests the fine mesh is within about 1% of a mesh-independent answer, so I used it for every case after this.

04 · Ride-height sweep

How close to the ground is too close?

I ran six ride heights from 25 to 150 mm, plus a free-air reference with the ground moved 5 m away. Ground effect more than doubles the downforce at 50 mm. Below that, the downforce collapses.

Efficiency (downforce ÷ drag)

L/D keeps rising with height

Peak downforce sits near 50 mm (h/c ≈ 0.2). Above it the curve falls gently: 4% lower at 75 mm. Below it the curve falls steeply: 29% lower at 25 mm, with 51% more drag.

The main plane drives the sensitivity. It depends most on the channel under the wing, falling from 1,047 N/m at 50 mm to 415 N/m in free air. The flaps peak at 75 mm and change far less.

Downforce and efficiency pull in opposite directions. L/D rises steadily with height, from 11.9 at 25 mm to 52.8 in free air. The lowest-drag setting is never the highest-downforce one.

Why downforce collapses at 25 mm

The bottom row colours x-velocity from −5 to +5 m/s, so blue means air moving backwards. At 25 mm, the merged wake behind the flaps reverses over a region about 0.6–0.8 m long. At 50 mm the wake slows but keeps moving forwards, and in free air it hardly slows at all. The flow on the wing surfaces stays attached in every case. This is off-surface flow reversal, sometimes called wake bursting: the stronger adverse pressure gradient near the ground stalls the wake itself.

05 · Design study

Changing one thing: flap 1 angle

At 50 mm, I rotated flap 1 by ±5° about its leading edge. In the 25° case I moved flap 2 by the same amount, so the slot 2 gap stayed as it was in the baseline.

The baseline is close to the downforce optimum: rotating flap 1 either way loses downforce.

15° is the efficiency setting. It gives up 5.6% of the downforce for 16% less drag. The main plane loses the most, even though I didn't touch it, because a less-loaded flap does less to pull the flow under the main plane. The elements work as a system.

25° is worse both ways. Flap 1 makes less downforce for more drag, and flap 2 loses 14% because it sits in flap 1's thicker, slower wake.

06 · 3D

What 2D can't show: tip vortices

Finally I modelled the 800 mm half-span wing with its endplate at 50 mm, using a symmetry plane and mirroring the result. The student licence capped the mesh at 875k cells, so these numbers are indicative rather than precise. The trends are clear though.

Pressure leaks round the endplate from the high-pressure upper surface to the low-pressure underside, forming the tip vortex. That costs 36% of the downforce per metre and adds induced drag, so drag more than doubles. The main plane loses most because its ground-effect suction can now escape sideways. In 2D its drag is negative (the suction pulls it forwards); in 3D it turns slightly positive.

07 · Outcome

What I learned and what's next

Findings

  • A validated 2D k-ω SST setup tracks the S1223 lift slope within 9%, with an offset that the turbulent-boundary-layer assumption explains.
  • At its best ride height (50 mm, h/c ≈ 0.2), ground effect gives the wing 2.27× its free-air downforce.
  • Too close to the ground, the wake reverses and downforce falls 29%. Peak downforce and peak efficiency sit at different heights.
  • The baseline flap angle is near the downforce optimum. Flattening flap 1 by 5° raises efficiency by 13%.
  • In 3D, tip vortices remove about a third of the 2D downforce and more than double the drag.

Limitations & next steps

  • Rerun with Transition SST to capture laminar flow and close the gap to XFoil.
  • Add 35 and 40 mm cases to pin down exactly where the wake starts to reverse.
  • The 3D mesh is coarse (y⁺ ≈ 20–50) because of the licence limit. A finer mesh would firm up the 3D numbers.
  • Use the 3D pressure loads for an FEA check of wing bending.
  • Next project: a full car model in SolidWorks Flow Simulation.
